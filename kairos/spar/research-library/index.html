Identifying function-relevant, sequence-agnostic features in a protein model using sparse autoencoders

  • Best Lightning Talk
  • Spring 2026

Isha HarrisMentored by Gary Abel

Biological AI models are powerful tools for detecting biological threats, but it remains unclear whether their predictions generalize to highly non-natural proteins. This is increasingly important as AI-enabled biodesign produces novel sequences that may not be reliably captured by direct sequence-comparison approaches used in DNA synthesis screening. Here, we use sparse autoencoder (SAE) features from protein language model activations to test whether interpretable internal representations can identify structural similarity across generated proteins. We profile residue-level SAE activations across proteins with varying sequence identity and predicted structural similarity to a wild-type reference, and evaluate whether aggregated feature representations distinguish predicted structure-preserving from structure-altered designs. The results show that SAE features can identify structure-preserving proteins even at low sequence similarity, with wild-type-like activation patterns persisting despite substantial sequence divergence. This suggests that interpretability methods may help recover biologically meaningful signals from artificially designed proteins, supporting more robust, function-aware approaches to biological threat detection.

Do steering vectors transfer across model scales?

  • Spring 2026

Marcus AliyevMentored by Katja Grace

Steering vectors offer fine-grained control over a model’s behaviour without retraining it, but nearly every published vector is fitted to a single model at a single size. We fit steering vectors on the smallest member of three open model families and test them on every larger member, measuring both the intended behavioural shift and the collateral damage to unrelated capabilities. Transfer holds within roughly one order of magnitude of parameters and degrades sharply beyond it, with the failure mode being loss of specificity rather than loss of strength.

Compute thresholds as a governance lever: what the evidence supports

  • Fall 2025

Priya RamanMentored by Janika Schmitt

Compute thresholds are the lever most current AI regulation reaches for, but the training-compute figures behind them are self-reported, inconsistently defined, and rarely audited. We reconstruct compute estimates for eighteen recent frontier training runs from public disclosures, hardware availability and inference-cost data, and compare them against the thresholds written into current rules. Estimates vary by up to a factor of four depending on defensible methodological choices, which is enough to move several runs across a regulatory line.

Sandbagging detection under distribution shift

  • Fall 2025

Tomás HerreraMentored by Dawn Song

A model that underperforms deliberately on an evaluation is hard to distinguish from one that is simply weak on that distribution. We build a benchmark of paired capability and evaluation tasks that differ only in framing, and test whether existing sandbagging detectors survive a shift in task domain. Detectors calibrated on one domain lose most of their discriminative power on another, suggesting current results overstate how ready these methods are for deployment.

Measuring the labour-market footprint of coding assistants

  • Spring 2025

Wen LiMentored by Yiyou Sun

We link job-posting data to the release timeline of widely used coding assistants and estimate changes in demand for junior software roles across markets with differing adoption rates. The estimated effect is concentrated in postings that describe routine implementation work, and is not detectable in postings that emphasise system design.
