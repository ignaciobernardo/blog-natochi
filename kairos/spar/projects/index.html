About the project

The project. Language models encode abstract concepts like time geometrically. In prior work (arXiv:2606.05194), we found that a model’s time horizon, from seconds to centuries, forms an ordinal manifold in its residual stream, and that activations at change-of-turn tokens carry both this geometry and the model’s temporal preference. That study covered single decisions.

The question. This project asks whether the same structure governs planning: as a model decomposes a task into steps across a multi-turn conversation, can the time horizon it is planning over be predicted from its activations? Both myopic and scheming failures are failures of how far ahead a model plans, and a horizon we can read from activations is a horizon we can monitor.

The plan. We build a dataset generator and an activation-capture pipeline, validate it on small models, then run the largest dense Qwen model that fits available hardware plus matched open-weight families. The deliverable is a go/no-go signal on planning-time temporal geometry, enough to justify scaling toward horizon monitors and steering.

Theory of change

Time horizon shapes decisions. Options are scored by temporal scope: a cost unacceptable over a week can be right over a decade. Time preference is fundamental to planning and cooperation, where an agent accepts a present cost for a later shared payoff. The wrong horizon yields systematically wrong decisions even when values are right.

Alignment is partly temporal: it needs sound objectives and the right horizon. The target is to detect and hold that horizon while it is still tractable.

Activation geometry can act as a fail-safe. If the planning horizon is legible in activations, we can characterize its geometry, monitor representations against that manifold at inference, and correct drift.

  • Myopia: over-weighting the immediate objective cuts corners and produces short-horizon reward seeking that worsens with task length.
  • Scheming: a long-horizon planner can hide its plan under oversight while pursuing a misaligned goal later.
  1. Existence. Does multi-turn planning carry stable temporal geometry at boundary tokens? Deliverable: the evidence.
  2. Monitors. If so, train probes reporting a plan’s horizon per turn, cheaper than token-by-token chain-of-thought analysis.
  3. Control. If readable, steer drifting representations back to the intended horizon without retraining.

Your role

Mentees will lead the day-to-day technical work. The balance of autonomy and direction will be calibrated to each mentee’s preference and experience.

Prerequisites

  • Highly proficient in Python.
  • Have run local LLM inference with PyTorch and Hugging Face transformers, including loading checkpoints, chat templates, generation, and hybrid API-only usage.
  • Have extracted and inspected internal activations from a transformer at least once using hooks, nnsight, TransformerLens, or an equivalent tool.
  • Comfortable with the linear algebra behind PCA, projections, eigendecomposition, and basic 2D/3D embedding plots.
  • Able to commit at least 10 hours per week for the full duration and work independently between meetings.

Application questions

  1. Suppose our first runs show no clean horizon structure at change-of-turn tokens. Propose two concrete follow-up experiments that would distinguish “no signal” from “confounded signal” and “signal at a different location.”
  2. The observed geometry could reflect the literal horizon wording in the prompt rather than an internal planning representation. Describe one control experiment that separates these explanations.
  3. Name one method other than PCA you would use to detect or quantify horizon structure in the activations, and explain why it would be a better choice.
