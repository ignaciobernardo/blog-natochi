What we're changing for cohort 02

Cohort 01 taught us four things. Here is what changes when about 20 fellows meet in the Bay Area in January.

Cohort 02 starts with a week in person in the Bay Area (San Francisco or Berkeley), January 23–31, 2027. It will be about 20 fellows, up from 7. Applications are not open yet.

Four changes

  • 01Longer selectionOutreach starts two to three months ahead, and every candidate does at least two interviews and a take-home.
  • 02Time to say yesA much longer window to accept, get employer authorization and sort visas.
  • 03PodsTwenty fellows split into small groups that work and meet together, so the peer dynamic that worked at seven holds.
  • 04Employer trackA pilot of accelerated interviews and project ideas hiring orgs accept as work trials. M3 stays independent, not a hiring pipeline.

What stays the same

A paid month on your own project, alongside your job, at your rate up to $100 an hour. A weekly standup with three peers and one facilitator keeps the accountability tight. Nobody directs your work.

Eight open problems in AI security

Short briefs on compute governance, inference verification, model attestation and 5 more, with where to start on each.

You pick the direction. These are the problems we think are most neglected relative to how much they matter.

01 · Compute governance

Training compute is the one input to frontier AI you can physically count. Nobody has built the accounting for it.

Policy proposals lean on compute thresholds as the trigger for almost everything: reporting duties, licensing, export controls. The thresholds are written. The mechanisms that would make them mean anything are not.

  • 01A credible chip-inventory scheme that a regulator could audit
  • 02Metering that survives an operator who wants to under-report
  • 03What a threshold means once training is distributed

02 · Inference verification

When an API returns an answer, you have no way to check which model produced it.

A provider can silently route you to a smaller model, a quantised one, or the same weights behind a different system prompt. You would not notice, and no deployed system gives you the means to find out.

  • 01Cryptographic proofs of which weights served a request
  • 02Statistical fingerprinting that holds under adversarial serving
  • 03What a verifiable-inference guarantee should even claim

03 · Model attestation

There is no code signature for weights.

Given a model file, you cannot establish who trained it, on what data, under which safety process, or whether it has been modified since it left the lab. The provenance chain that we take for granted in software supply chains simply does not exist here.

  • 01Signing and provenance formats for weights and checkpoints
  • 02Detecting post-release tampering and fine-tune drift
  • 03Binding a training process to the artefact it produced

04 · Hardware security

Every software guarantee eventually bottoms out in an accelerator you did not build.

Confidential computing on GPUs is where compute governance, weight security and inference verification all have to land. The attack surface is barely mapped: firmware, side channels, physical extraction, and the trust you implicitly place in one vendor's attestation roots.

  • 01Breaking or hardening GPU confidential-computing modes
  • 02Side-channel and physical-extraction work on accelerators
  • 03What happens when the attestation root itself is the adversary

05 · Securing model weights

A frontier model is a few terabytes that a state intelligence service would like to walk out with.

Defending them is an insider-threat and exfiltration problem at a scale most security teams have never had to run: thousands of people with legitimate access, research velocity as an explicit competing goal, and adversaries at the top of the capability ladder.

  • 01Exfiltration paths that survive a cooperative insider
  • 02Access control that does not collapse under research velocity
  • 03Making a published security level actually verifiable

06 · Treaty verification

Any international agreement on AI needs parties to check compliance without opening their labs to each other.

That is a technical question before it is a diplomatic one. Remote attestation, hardware-enforced limits, privacy-preserving audit — the agreement can only promise what some mechanism can check.

  • 01Verification schemes that reveal compliance and nothing else
  • 02Hardware-enforced limits a counterparty would accept
  • 03Which commitments are checkable at all, and which are theatre

07 · Agent security

Agents read untrusted text and then act with real credentials.

Prompt injection is the case everyone names, and it is the easy one. The harder problems are delegation, revocation, and containment once an agent is several steps into a plan it formed itself.

  • 01Real trust boundaries between an agent and its tools
  • 02Delegation and revocation for credentials an agent holds
  • 03Containment and audit after an agent goes off-plan

08 · Authorization against misuse

Deciding what a given user may get out of a model is an access-control problem wearing a safety costume.

Refusal training is a blunt instrument applied at the wrong layer: it treats every request as anonymous and every user as identical, then tries to guess intent from the text alone.

  • 01Graded capability authorization instead of blanket refusal
  • 02Identity and accountability that survive resale and proxying
  • 03Audit trails that answer who asked for what, afterwards

A week in London, alongside AISB

Mornings reading the field, afternoons letting a small group try to break your hypothesis.

Cohort 01's first week ran London, Aug 30 – Sep 4, 2026, alongside AISB.

In person. Mornings are research blocks: you read the field and write down what is missing. Afternoons are discussion sprints, with field experts there to red-team the parts you cannot see.

Why alongside AISB

M3 is a collaboration between Kairos, Constellation and AISB. M3's first in-person week runs alongside AISB London, giving fellows from both programs access to each other.

What it costs you

Nothing. We cover flights, hotel, food and anything else you need to be there. Your salary starts after that week, when the build phase begins.

How the pay works

Paid for the hours you put into your project, at your rate, up to $100 an hour.

You are paid for the hours you put into your project, at your rate, capped at $100 an hour. The build phase starts with one paid month; projects that need more time can extend by two more.

Keeping your job

No. The build phase is remote and runs alongside your job, so you decide how many hours to commit. The only in-person block is the first week, in London.

After demo day

Papers get routed to workshops and arXiv referrals, tools go to the open source community, and new orgs get introductions to funders. Projects that need more time can extend by two paid months.

The researcher, the founder, the field builder

Three kinds of senior security people the fellowship is built around.

The researcher

You push the frontier of AI security. You have a question the field has not answered, a tool AI security needs, or a direction that needs to be explored.

The founder

You see a gap worth an organisation. A non-profit that governments will need and nobody has built yet. You want the time to scope it, test whether it holds, and leave with something fundable.

The field builder

You want to scale up AI security. You have spent years inside the area and the bottleneck you see is people, not ideas. You want to build the thing that widens the door.

Off the list is fine: what counts as a project

Empirical or theoretical, on our list or yours. A survey of other people's work does not count.

Empirical or theoretical

Both count. A benchmark nobody had built and a harness that makes an existing claim testable are both outputs we can route onward.

A survey of work other people already did does not count.

Off this list is fine

The list is where we think the gaps are. If you have spent a decade inside a problem we did not think to name, that is a better reason to work on it than anything written here.

Bring it in week one and let the group try to break it.
