03

Eight open problems

or bring your own

Training compute is the one input to frontier AI you can physically count. Nobody has built the accounting for it.

  • A credible chip-inventory scheme that a regulator could audit
  • Metering that survives an operator who wants to under-report
  • What a threshold means once training is distributed

When an API returns an answer, you have no way to check which model produced it.

  • Cryptographic proofs of which weights served a request
  • Statistical fingerprinting that holds under adversarial serving
  • What a verifiable-inference guarantee should even claim

There is no code signature for weights.

  • Signing and provenance formats for weights and checkpoints
  • Detecting post-release tampering and fine-tune drift
  • Binding a training process to the artefact it produced

Every software guarantee eventually bottoms out in an accelerator you did not build.

  • Breaking or hardening GPU confidential-computing modes
  • Side-channel and physical-extraction work on accelerators
  • What happens when the attestation root itself is the adversary

A frontier model is a few terabytes that a state intelligence service would like to walk out with.

  • Exfiltration paths that survive a cooperative insider
  • Access control that does not collapse under research velocity
  • Making a published security level actually verifiable

Any international agreement on AI needs parties to check compliance without opening their labs to each other.

  • Verification schemes that reveal compliance and nothing else
  • Hardware-enforced limits a counterparty would accept
  • Which commitments are checkable at all, and which are theatre

Agents read untrusted text and then act with real credentials.

  • Real trust boundaries between an agent and its tools
  • Delegation and revocation for credentials an agent holds
  • Containment and audit after an agent goes off-plan

Deciding what a given user may get out of a model is an access-control problem wearing a safety costume.

  • Graded capability authorization instead of blanket refusal
  • Identity and accountability that survive resale and proxying
  • Audit trails that answer who asked for what, afterwards

05

Questions

Q01How does the pay work?

You are paid for the hours you put into your project, at your rate, capped at $100 an hour. The build phase starts with one paid month; projects that need more time can extend by two more.

Q02What does the first week cost me?

Nothing. We cover flights, hotel, food and anything else you need to be there. Your salary starts after that week, when the build phase begins.

Q03Do I have to quit my job?

No. The build phase is remote and runs alongside your job, so you decide how many hours to commit. The only in-person block is the first week, in London.

Q04Do I need a project going in?

No. Week one exists to find it: mornings reading the field, afternoons letting a small group and field experts try to break your hypothesis. The problems list is where we think the gaps are, and off-list is fine.

Q05Who directs the work?

Nobody. A weekly standup with three peers and one facilitator keeps the accountability tight, but the direction stays yours.

Q06What happens after demo day?

Papers get routed to workshops and arXiv referrals, tools go to the open source community, and new orgs get introductions to funders. Projects that need more time can extend by two paid months.

Q07Who is behind M3?

A collaboration between Constellation, Kairos and AISB. Anything this page does not answer: fernando@m3fellowship.com.

Q08What's your relationship with AISB?

M3 is a collaboration between Kairos, Constellation and AISB. M3's first in-person week runs alongside AISB London, giving fellows from both programs access to each other.
