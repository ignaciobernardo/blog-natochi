What is SPAR?

SPAR is a fully-remote program pairing fellows with professionals addressing risks from AI to work together on impactful projects. Fellows get guidance, and mentors get world-class talent for their projects.

  • Mentors can choose to commit anywhere from 2-10 hours/week.
  • The program runs for 3 months with options for extension, culminating in a Demo Day attended by top AI safety orgs.
  • We look for mentors working on large-scale risks from advanced AI, with a strong theory of change for how their project tackles them. Past mentors include organization leaders and faculty, as well as early-career researchers whose SPAR teams did excellent work.

What kinds of projects does SPAR host?

SPAR supports a broad range of projects and research that we think is helpful for mitigating the risks posed by advanced AI, including but not limited to:

We are actively looking for more work on

Misalignment science

Understanding how and why models end up misaligned, rather than tuning a metric on current systems.

Examples:

  • Explaining observed failures, such as why some models fake alignment and others don't
  • Showing a safety method is weaker than claimed, for example chain-of-thought obfuscation or eval awareness inflating safety scores
  • Finding effects our current frameworks don't predict, such as emergent misalignment
  • Working out how to measure things like reward-seeking
  • Building realistic model organisms (deliberately misaligned models we can study)
AI policy

Policy work that reduces large-scale risks from advanced AI at the federal, state and international levels. We prioritize mentors with a clear theory of change for how policy changes and who the key stakeholders are. Our top priorities are ambitious US domestic policy and US-China work.

Examples:

  • Drafting legislative proposals and national security analysis on pacing frontier AI development
  • Research that supports safety-case review, export control enforcement and model weight security
  • US-China work, international agreements and arms control, the role of middle powers, and compute monitoring and hardware verification (EU policy projects are also welcome)
  • Drafting, analyzing and implementing state AI legislation
  • Policy briefs and explainers that make research usable for its audience
  • Research that produces public evidence of emerging risks
Macrostrategy and AGI preparedness

Conceptual and strategic research on getting through the transition to advanced AI well.

Examples:

  • Dynamics of an intelligence explosion
  • AI-enabled concentration of power
  • Institutions for a world with advanced AI
AI incident detection

Finding, reporting and analyzing harmful or misaligned AI behavior in the wild.

Examples:

  • Monitoring agent transcripts and logs at scale
  • Forensic analysis of AI incidents
  • Incident reporting systems and databases
  • Early-warning signals for new failure types
Communications and public understanding

Communicating the state of AI and its risks clearly and accurately, to the public and other key audiences.

Examples:

  • In-depth public reports on notable AI incidents
  • Video explainers that make technical safety results accessible
  • Explainers on frontier AI companies' policy positions and lobbying
AI character and model values

What values, dispositions and personas models have, how training shapes them, how stable they are, and how this might carry over to future models.

Examples:

  • Model specs
  • Persona drift
  • Character training and its side effects

We are also excited about

Theory of learning and generalization

Mathematical accounts of what training produces and why, aimed at predictions or guarantees that will hold for future systems.

Examples:

  • Singular learning theory and developmental interpretability
  • Heuristic explanations and low-probability estimation
  • Computational mechanics of internal representations
  • Theoretical guarantees for oversight methods such as debate
Multi-agent safety

Risks that only show up when many AI agents act together, which testing one model at a time misses. We prioritize work tied to catastrophic outcomes over abstract game-theory setups and agent security problems such as prompt injection.

Examples:

  • Collusion between AI agents, including monitors colluding with the agents they oversee and hidden coordination through steganography
  • Catching sabotage spread across many small actions by many agents
  • Dangerous capabilities or behaviors that emerge when many agents interact, and how to detect them early
Hardware-enabled verification and compute governance

Technical tools that let parties verify claims about AI development without having to trust each other.

Examples:

  • On-chip mechanisms that attest how AI chips are used
  • Location and usage attestation
  • Cryptography for verifying training runs and model properties
  • Privacy-preserving audits
Securing model weights

Protecting model weights and AI infrastructure, and making models robust to adversarial misuse.

Examples:

  • Securing weights and algorithms against theft by well-resourced attackers
  • Red-teaming lab infrastructure
  • Preventing distillation
AI uplift for safety research

Using AI agents to do safety research, and checking that their output can be trusted.

Examples:

  • Training interventions that differentially speed up alignment work
  • Methods for red-teaming the results of automated alignment work
  • Failure modes of automated alignment work
  • Detecting sandbagging (deliberate underperformance) on safety tasks
Safety cases and third-party auditing

Methods that let people outside a lab check its claims that a model is safe to train further or deploy.

Examples:

  • Adversarially reviewing safety cases
  • Verifying what labs report, such as compute use and evaluation results
  • Auditing games that test whether an audit catches a deliberately hidden problem
Standards and lab accountability

Analyzing how frontier AI companies manage risk and designing better standards. Each project should name who will use the work and what decision or practice it aims to change.

Examples:

  • Reviews of frontier safety policies, corporate governance structures and model cards
  • Risk management analyses that compare companies' practices
  • Public assessments and trackers that rate companies against safety standards
Digital minds

Model welfare, moral status and AI introspection.

Examples:

  • Methods for evaluating whether model self-reports are reliable
  • Welfare-relevant behaviors and preferences in models
  • Frameworks for moral status under uncertainty
Funding strategy

Analyzing the value of interventions in AI safety and surfacing new or underrated ones.

Examples:

  • Economic analysis and design of funding mechanisms
  • Supporting grant investigations

We're also happy to consider projects outside these lists.

Host projects as an organization

Organizations can now apply once for everyone on their team who wants to mentor, instead of each mentor applying on their own. Your mentors then select part-time or full-time fellows from around 100 applications per project, each already reviewed with standardized work tests and AI evaluations.

A point of contact at your organization fills out one form, listing who wants to mentor, roughly how much time each can give, and the broad directions their projects might take. These descriptions can be less detailed than a standard mentor application. SPAR is part-time by default, but fellows on your projects can also work full-time, and your organization can choose to offer them a stipend.

  • Applicant reach

    Our Fall 2026 round drew nearly 6,000 applicants, including more senior talent than ever, and we expect more for Spring 2027.

  • Application review

    Applications reach your mentors with standardized work test results and AI evaluations to help them screen, and mentors can request interviews and reference checks.

  • No pre-commitment

    Nobody commits in advance to taking fellows, and each mentor decides whether any applicant meets their bar.

Why apply?

  • Project support

    Gain access to qualified applicants to advance your projects. Who you choose to work with is entirely up to you, and there's no commitment to take on any fellows if none are a good fit.

  • Funding

    SPAR projects can apply for grants of up to $30k for expenses like compute and API access.

  • Find long-term collaborators

    Many SPAR mentors end up inviting their fellows back to work on additional projects after the program ends, or hiring them to their teams.

  • Build your field

    Help more skilled people move into crucial work like yours.

Am I eligible?

We typically accept applications from people with AI safety or biosecurity experience matching or exceeding that of a late PhD student or a MATS scholar. We're excited to receive applications in technical AI safety, policy and governance, biosecurity, strategy, communications and fieldbuilding.

If you are a senior graduate student, postdoc, academic faculty member, or professional at a lab, you can suggest any project within SPAR's focus areas. If you are a junior graduate student or a MATS scholar, your project should be adjacent to your existing research focus.

If you don't fit any of these categories, it must be very clear that you have legible credentials to lead the project you propose. We accept undergraduate applicants to mentor only in exceptional circumstances.

FAQ

Is my project a good fit?

Your project may be a good fit if:

  • It can be scoped for 3 months of work at 5-40 hr/week/fellow, at a team size you're comfortable supervising
  • It has a strong theory of change for tackling large-scale risks from advanced AI
  • It's focused on AI technical safety, AI policy and governance, AI strategy, AI security, biosecurity, communications or fieldbuilding

For biosecurity projects, we don't require projects to be focused on AI risks specifically, but we do require their theories of change to potentially be helpful in preventing or mitigating AI-enabled bioattacks (exclusively or in addition to other threats). Given the remote nature of the program, we typically won't accept projects involving laboratory work.

How qualified are typical SPAR applicants?

Fall 2026 had about 6,000 applicants, roughly a third each undergraduates, graduate students and mid-career professionals. Acceptance rate was about 14%.

  • Graduate student with strong publication record and 2-4 years research experience in ML or in technology policy
  • Top-tier undergraduate with research internships at major companies or labs
  • Mid-career professional with 5-10 years in software or data science, transitioning to AI safety

We expect applicants to have some relevant technical background to the projects at hand, but not all projects will receive applicants with deep research experience.

What if I don't get any applicants I'm satisfied with?

You can just withdraw from the program. Submitting a SPAR project doesn't create any commitment to move forward unless you find mentees you're excited about. However, we find withdrawals are rare.

How will mentees be assigned to my project?

Mentees will apply to your project and answer any custom application questions you've included. You'll review applications, including scheduling any interviews or trial tasks you'd like. Please note you may need to set aside substantial time for application review—in Fall 2025, the median project received 62 applications, while the top 20% received over 100.

After reviewing applications, you'll send us a ranked shortlist of candidates, and applicants will also submit a preference ranking among projects they've applied to. We'll run an algorithm that matches mentees with projects based on both mentor and mentee preferences, as well as other factors like ensuring no one gets assigned more hours of work than they're available for. While this means you're not guaranteed to be matched with any given applicant, we will never assign you mentees who weren't on your shortlist.

What does SPAR expect of mentors?
  • Review mentee applications by SPAR's deadlines, including arranging any interviews or assessments you're interested in. (The SPAR team will provide resources and guidance for managing this process.)
  • Coordinate weekly meetings with your project team.
  • Have weekly or biweekly 1:1s with each of your mentees. (There's also an option to give high-level asynchronous feedback only, though our bar for accepting these projects is higher.)
  • Be reasonably accessible to your mentees over a platform like Slack, Discord, Signal, Whatsapp, etc.
  • Set up a task management system to coordinate your mentees' to-dos.
  • Anything else that's needed to ensure your project stays on track.

The mentor application asks you to commit to a level of involvement per mentee, from 5hr/wk/mentee to high-level asynchronous feedback. Whatever commitment you make, we ask that you honor it.

How many mentees can my project take on?

SPAR projects can have as few as one mentee or as many as you'd like. If you accept a particularly large team, the SPAR team may check in to ensure you can commit appropriate time to supervising each mentee.

Can there be more than one mentor on a project?

Yes, mentors are welcome to team up on projects!

Can one mentor run multiple projects?

Yes! You can run as many SPAR projects as you'd like, as long as you can commit appropriate time to your mentees. You can also submit many project proposals and later decide which ones to go forward with, based on the received interest and quality of applications.
